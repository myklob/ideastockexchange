Interest Validity Debate: [the interest, as a need with a subject]

A validity question is a claim. "This interest should be honored," "this interest matters more than that one," and "in this standoff, safety outranks privacy" are beliefs with reasons to agree and disagree, scored the way every other belief on this site is scored. This page is that pro and con skeleton applied to one interest's validity at three scopes. It is to the Validity score what a belief page is to a single argument: the place it gets its own tree.

When to use it: when the Validity score is itself the fight. The interest's own page carries both scores side by side and is enough while nobody disputes them; expand to this page when validity is contested, or when the same interest is listed by several beliefs and they all need to read one number.

The Interest and the Ground Rules

The interest: [who needs what, e.g. "current homeowners need to protect the value of their homes"]
Listed by: [the belief pages that list it, each a link, and the side each lists it on]
Measured by: [what reading would show the need is met or unmet]
Opening prior: [the Maslow rung and its band, e.g. Safety, 70 to 85]

  • Maslow sets the opening price; the arguments do the trading. The interest starts at its rung's band and the reasons below move it up or down. The band is a prior, not a verdict and not a lookup table: an esteem need finishes above a safety need when its arguments are stronger.
  • Both sides are rational. People on each side hold their interest for reasons tied to a real need, not because they are stupid or evil. Whether the need is really theirs is the other track's question; here it is taken as real and asked whether it should be honored.
  • Arguments set the score, nothing else. Validity traces entirely to the scored reasons below. It is never set by who holds the interest, how many of them there are, how much power they have, or how loudly they assert it. Cross that line and the engine rebuilds "might makes right" inside the machine built to replace it.
  • Feasibility is a different track. How hard the need is to satisfy, and what each side can do without a deal, are read elsewhere and multiply into nothing here.

What Belongs on the Other Track: the Alibi Test

Kept here so a contributor knows what does not go on this page. Whether the side really holds this motive is settled by its record, on the interest's own page: the candidate interest that predicts the side's votes, spending and past positions with the fewest exceptions wins. A neighborhood letter says traffic; the same association backed a supermarket with more car trips and fought a townhouse project that came with a garage; "homeowners need to protect the value of their homes" predicts all three and "traffic" predicts one. Run the same test on your own side. Neither finding is an insult; a prediction that fails is a motive misread.

Raises Linkage AccuracyLowers Linkage Accuracy
Votes, spending, donations and effort go where this interest says they shouldThe side acts against what this interest would predict
Members name it themselves, in surveys and on the recordA rival interest predicts the same record with fewer exceptions
The same group takes the same side in other debates where only this interest is at stakeThe behavior has another documented cause, so it is not evidence for this one
Analysts with no stake in the outcome find the same patternThe attribution rests on rhetoric alone, with no behavioral record either way

Prevalence, the share of the side that each interest actually drives, is recorded beside the interest from surveys, with a source. It is a reading, not a score, and it multiplies into nothing.

The Mirror Test: Six Criteria

"I need the street in front of my house kept free of other people's cars." Real? Certainly. Valid? Hold it up to a mirror. Would you accept the same rule enforced on you by your neighbor? And if everyone claimed the street in front of their house, where would anyone park? The interest is real and fails anyway. "I need to get to work in under an hour" passes both tests. Only one of these is a need a resolution should be built around, and it is not the louder one. Every reason in the three scopes below names the criterion it leans on.

CriterionThe questionAn interest fails it when
ReciprocityWould the side accept this interest honored for its opponents?It wants a rule for others it would reject for itself
Universal testWhat happens if everyone pursues it?Universal pursuit makes it impossible for anyone, or guarantees perpetual conflict
Asks no loss from othersCan it be met without someone else losing?It is satisfied only at another party's expense, or it is a claim on other people's property or choices
Alternative satisfactionCan the underlying need be met another way?Substitutes exist and the side rejects them, which usually means the stated need is not the real one
Scope and reversibilityHow many people, how badly, and can it be undone?Ignoring it costs little, spreads thin, or is easily reversed
Unmet and sharedIs the need actually unmet, and does anyone outside the asking group recognize it?The status quo already satisfies it, or it is held by few and recognized nowhere else

Where the Score Starts: the Maslow Rungs

Every interest starts from the band of the need it serves, and the argument tree moves it from there. The same need can appear on several rungs depending on how it is written, and the mirror test treats the versions very differently.

RungOpening priorInterests written as needs
Physiological85 to 100People need food, water, shelter, sleep and medical care. Workers need wages that cover them. Patients need treatment they can reach.
Safety70 to 85Protection from violence, crime and war; an income they can count on, savings that hold their value, a legal order that applies tomorrow the way it did today. Financial interests sit here: "homeowners need to protect the value of their homes", "renters need a lease that cannot be cancelled on a whim".
Belonging50 to 70A family, a community, a congregation, a country and a team to belong to. Every form of group solidarity. The need to belong is valid; what the group does to outsiders is a separate interest, scored separately, and often below the floor.
Esteem40 to 60To be respected, heard and credited. Officials need to be seen as honest; voters need to be answered; workers need their effort recognized. Status needs fail the mirror test: status is scarce by definition.
Self-actualization30 to 50To grow, create, learn, worship and choose for themselves. Low prior, not low value: these lose to survival when the two collide and win on their own arguments when they do not.
Curiosity and play30 to 50To understand, to be entertained, to feel excitement. Explains motives the political ones miss (conspiracy theories as thrill and belonging; voting for disruption because watching the system break is more interesting). The need is real; the vehicle is scored on the belief page.
Below the floor0 to 20Not a rung. Interests that fail the criteria outright: domination (our group needs the other group kept down), tribal winning (we need them to lose), pretext (a belonging interest worn as a disguise), and manufactured needs that exist only if a false claim is true.

Bad Motives Are Scored, Not Deleted

The most useful row in any interest table is the one that is real and illegitimate. In the government-ethics beliefs the opponents' list includes "some officeholders want to keep using their position and their families to make money." Its Drive ties for highest on that side; its validity is the lowest on the page. It ranks first as an explanation of behavior and last as something a resolution should honor. That is the information, and this page is where the low validity gets argued rather than assumed. The same move must be made against your own side, or it is propaganda: "our side needs to win the next election" belongs on both lists of any partisan belief, with the same low validity on both.

Where the Validity Score Goes

Two sisters want the same orange; the parent cuts it in half; one needed juice, the other peel. Positions collided; interests never did. The validity argued on this page is what the orange arithmetic reads.

OutputHow the scores set it
Shared interestsInterests on opposite sides whose statements say the same thing (paired by similarity, then confirmed or disputed by people), with validity at or above the Resolution Floor of 70 on both sides; the pair's combined validity is the harmonic mean. Compromise is built here first.
Primary Conflict PairThe highest-Drive interest on each side that is not shared, staged head to head: the one disagreement actually driving the debate. Drive uses validity on purpose: a pretext cannot be the primary conflict, and neither can a saintly interest nobody is moved by.
Solution scoresA candidate solution earns, for each interest it satisfies, the fraction of the interest it meets (0 to 1) times the interest's validity, summed per side; the overall score is 2 x S x O / (S + O). Half an orange each: 0.40. Peel to the baker and juice to the drinker: 0.80. The whole orange to one sister: zero, because a solution that satisfies only one side has resolved nothing. Interests below the floor earn a solution nothing even when it satisfies them. No interest counts extra because its holders are powerful.
Argument importanceEvery reason on a belief page speaks to one or more interests, and its importance is set by the most valid interest it genuinely bears on: validity x how directly the reason bears on it, taking the largest. A true fact that matters to nobody weighs nothing.
Who gains, who paysEvery cost and benefit on a belief page names the interest that collects it or pays it. A cost always has an address.
