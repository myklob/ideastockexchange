Idea Stock Exchange

A prediction market for ideas, where arguments are scored, not just shouted.

A claim is scored from the arguments and evidence beneath it (ReasonRank), recursively, with every factor read from the page that argues it. A second layer, a Market Price set by what people will bet, is designed and not yet built; where this page describes it, read it as a plan. The live belief pages are the part that exists, and every number on them is computed at build time from two tables.

Browse the scored belief pages: five beliefs on government ethics across 261 pages, argued on both sides, every number computed from two tables at build time and linked to the page that produced it. Each page opens by saying what it supports doing, and all five currently say “not yet” and then say exactly what is missing.

Alongside them: the method, every rule on one page including eleven things the tool cannot do · what changed in this revision, in claims and in the scores they moved · and the data behind all of it as JSON, SQL and a loaded SQLite database.

The three pillars

ReasonRank

Each argument inherits strength from the arguments and evidence that support or undermine it — recursively. Strong claims earn their score; weak ones can’t hide behind volume.

Market Price (planned)

A belief’s share price would reflect what the crowd thinks is true, set by a constant-product market maker. Buy if you disagree with the price and can defend the position. Nothing on the live site implements this yet.

The arbitrage

When ReasonRank and Market Price diverge, there’s a trade. Logic-grounded conviction gets rewarded; popularity without reasoning does not.

How a belief page is built

Every belief decomposes the same way. The page is a navigation tool into a scored argument network, not an essay. There is no introductory paragraph — the reader goes straight from the claim to the structured pros and cons.

1
Belief statement One declarative sentence. The thing under debate.
2
Argument trees Reasons to Agree and Reasons to Disagree, side by side. Each entry is a complete, atomic proposition — a sentence that could headline a page of its own, because it does. A cell that cannot carry a truth score is not an argument.
3
Evidence ledger Findings that can fail empirically, each with its own page and its own source. What kind of source it is sets where the claim starts before anyone argues with it: a published statistic every replication confirms opens at 0.95, the same statistic contradicted opens at 0.05, and a claim citing nothing opens at a coin flip and contributes nothing.
4
Values, interests, assumptions Why supporters and opponents weigh things differently — and where their material interests are nudging their stated values.
5
Objective criteria The measurable thresholds that would change minds. This is where falsifiability lives.
6
Cost-benefit analysis Benefits vs. costs, then short-term vs. long-term. Quantified where data allows.
7
Resolution & biases Best compromise paths, primary obstacles, and the cognitive biases distorting each side.
8
Definitions Glossary at the bottom, never the top. The structured argument is what the reader came for.
Two layers, scored separately. Arguments are logical claims; evidence is empirical data. They fail differently and live in different sections. The rule that runs is sign × (2 × Truth − 1) × Confidence × Link × Imp × Uniq, signed so that a claim argued false counts against the side it was filed on and a claim nobody has argued counts exactly nothing. Every factor is read from the page that argues it, and the method page states all of it, including what the tool cannot do.

The ReasonRank formula

An argument’s contribution to its parent claim is a product of five factors and a sign. Every one of them is itself a page with its own argument, so there is no factor a reader cannot follow down to what set it:

  • Truth — the claim’s own score, entered as 2 × Truth − 1, so a claim argued false pulls the other way instead of merely pulling less.
  • Confidence — how much of the work behind that claim has been done. A claim nobody has argued passes nothing up, however true it looks.
  • Relevance (Linkage) — does the supporting argument actually bear on the conclusion?
  • Importance (Impact) — how much does the argument move the parent claim if true?
  • Uniqueness — is this a point the rows beside it have not already made? Without it, a claim repeated three ways counts three times.

contribution = sign × (2 × Truth − 1) × Confidence × Linkage × Importance × Uniqueness

Scoring is recursive: a belief’s score is computed from the scores of its sub-arguments, the way Google’s PageRank computed page authority from inbound links. A true-but-irrelevant argument scores near zero. An argument shown to be false does not score low, it scores against the side that filed it. And an argument nobody has supported scores exactly nothing, which is why a page whose whole graph cites no evidence sits at 0.50 however much is written beneath it.

The market price layer (designed, not built)

None of what follows runs anywhere. It is the design for a second layer, kept here because it is half the idea; the scored belief pages are the half that exists.

Independent of the logic score, users would invest virtual currency (IdeaCredits) in claims via a constant-product market maker. Buying YES shares pushes the price up; buying NO pushes it down. Prices reflect the crowd’s collective probability estimate — separately from whether the claim is logically sound.

When ReasonRank and Market Price diverge, rational actors profit:

ScenarioReasonRankMarket PriceActionRationale
UndervaluedHighLowBuy YES Logically supported claim the crowd hasn’t caught up to yet.
OvervaluedLowHighBuy NO Popular claim that doesn’t survive scrutiny.
Fairly valuedAlignedAlignedHold No edge.

Both numbers stay visible. No other platform shows you the gap and lets you bet on it closing.

The code

Two things live in the repository, and only one of them is published here.

The scored belief pages are generated by a Python toolchain in tools/static-site/ from two flat tables: one row per page, one row per row of every table on every page. No score is stored in either. The scorer, the evidence tiers, the confidence rule, the sensitivity sweep, the ReasonRank walk and the duplicate detector are one short module each, with a conformance corpus of twenty-four pages, their expected numbers, the constants and the tier weights published as data so an implementation in any other language can be held to the same contract without cloning anything. The content is kept as CSV as well as a spreadsheet, so a change to an argument diffs as a change to an argument rather than as a change in byte count.

The application is a TypeScript and Next.js codebase with a Prisma-backed schema for beliefs, arguments, evidence and linkage scores, and the market maker described above. It is a work in progress and a separate deployment. Its scoring engine does not yet follow the same rule as the published pages, which is an open question rather than an oversight.

Contribute

Three ways to help:

  • Developers: clone the repo, pick an issue labeled good first issue or help wanted. Priority areas: belief scoring pipeline, the Belief Equivalency Engine, and frontend belief display components.
  • Researchers and writers: add or improve a belief page. Arguments are scored by the five factors above, and each factor is argued on its own page rather than assigned. Evidence is classified by what kind of source it is, from the eighteen tiers the method page lists; the T1 to T4 scheme in the mock-ups above is not what the live pages use.
  • Everyone: star the repo. Submit a new belief as a GitHub issue. Join the discussion in GitHub Discussions.
